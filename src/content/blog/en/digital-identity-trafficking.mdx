As personal data, behavioral patterns, social networks, emotions and preferences, and even digital replicas of human beings increasingly become resources that can be digitized, commodified, packaged, traded, and monetized through technological advancement, should we have full rights to informed consent, control, and benefit-sharing over these aspects of ourselves?

## Case Study: A Ukrainian Woman's Digital Replica "Mass-Trafficked"

In 2023, a Ukrainian woman named Olga Loiek discovered on social platforms like Xiaohongshu and Bilibili that her appearance and voice had been "batch-used" across dozens of videos under different names and accounts. Even more concerning, these "people" bearing Olga Loiek's face—"Sofia," "Naqtasha," "April," and others—claimed to be "Russian girls" in the videos, promoting China and Sino-Russian friendship in fluent Mandarin Chinese, before eventually selling candy products.

For this series of "digital replica trafficking," Olga Loiek, as the rightful owner of that likeness and identity, had neither authorized nor been informed of any of this. Setting aside her failure to receive any corresponding benefit, the misrepresentation and public dissemination of her nationality, political stance, and personal identity constitutes a substantive violation of her dignity and right to self-determination.

## Gaps in Current Law: The Institutional Void in Personality Rights Protection

Under existing legal frameworks, victims may have civil claims for unjust enrichment or tortious conduct regarding commercial profits generated from misappropriated likenesses, such as merchandise revenue in the aforementioned case (though this remains subject to debate over legal elements). However, regarding personality rights protection, current legal structures do not clearly provide a direct basis for claiming damages or remedies.

Taking Taiwan's Civil Code as an example, Article 18 stipulates that victims of personality rights infringement may "claim damages or solatium only when specifically provided by law." Yet the types explicitly listed in the statute only cover body, health, reputation, freedom, credit, privacy, and chastity—they do not directly and explicitly encompass unauthorized use of facial features, voice, behavioral style, and other digital identity characteristics. Such infringements can only be tentatively covered through judicial interpretation of "other personality interests," but the scope of protection, elements, and legal effects remain unstable and inadequate.

## Digital Replicas as Part of Personality: The Essence of "Digital Human Trafficking"

In the Web 2.0 and Web 3.0 world, people's "digital replicas"—including appearance, voice, accounts, and virtual personas—have already become part of their personality; simultaneously, they constitute the "molecules" and "property" of the online world.

When someone's digital replica is collected, packaged, exploited, and sold without their full knowledge or ability to refuse, this is essentially and ethically no different from human trafficking in the physical world—treating humans as objects of transaction and priced commodities. In other words, using deepfake technology to generate and manipulate someone's face and voice without consent, especially for commercial or political exploitation, can be viewed as a new form of "digital human trafficking."

## Power Imbalance: Digital Exploitation Between Individuals and Platforms

This phenomenon also highlights the severe power and information asymmetry between users and platforms in the current digital environment. Individuals' digital replicas are highly vulnerable to exploitation and monetization in the online world, while the original personality bearers have almost no control or corresponding benefits.

Given this, we should not ignore that digital replicas have become constituent elements of personality and important resources in economic and social interactions. Legal frameworks should be designed to protect individuals' rights to informed consent, control, and benefit-sharing regarding their digital identity.

## International Legislative Trends: From Product Features to Dedicated Legislation

Recent developments in generative video AI have begun attempting to incorporate "digital characters" into institutionalized permission management. For example, product features have emerged allowing users to designate themselves or others as "reusable characters," with settings for usage scope, sharing permissions, and access boundaries for others.

Tennessee passed the Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security Act (also known as the "ELVIS Act") in 2024, explicitly bringing unauthorized AI-generated impersonation using someone's voice and likeness into the scope of civil and even criminal liability. Such efforts can serve as important reference directions for institutional design.

## From Human Trafficking to Digital Personality: The Same Fight for Freedom

Since the nineteenth century, humanity has fought for personal freedom and protested against human trafficking, establishing clear international law and human rights concepts. In today's virtual world, we face the very same fight for freedom—just "after personality has been digitized."

Returning to Taiwan's current legal framework, as mentioned above, Article 18 of the Civil Code adopts a "special provision reservation" structure for personality rights remedies. This means that when facial features, voice, behavioral style, and other "digital identity characteristics" are deepfake-generated, batch-misappropriated, or commercially monetized, victims often lack a clear, predictable, and readily applicable remedy path—even when they know they are being "copied and sold," they may fall into institutional disadvantage regarding evidence, jurisdiction, damage calculation, and immediate takedown.

## From Basic Act to Actionable Rights: The Next Step in Legal Reform

Notably, with the AI Basic Act having passed its third reading, the nation has established the direction and responsibility framework for AI governance on the foundation of "human-centered, fundamental rights protection." The government should now translate "the principles of the basic act" into "actionable rights and obligations."

Specifically, we should urgently promote:

- **Institutionalization of digital identity rights**: Explicitly codify rights over digital replicas (informed consent, authorization, withdrawal, traceability, benefit distribution)
- **Rapid remedy mechanisms**: Establish effective cease-and-desist and takedown mechanisms, evidence preservation, and accountability tracing
- **Responsibility allocation standards**: Propose enforceable standards and sanctions for the division of obligations among platforms, model providers, and users

If we allow "digital personality" to be batch-copied and traded through institutional gaps, the rights protection proclaimed by the basic act will remain mere principles, unable to become a reliable defense for the people.

## Conclusion: A Legal Reform Imperative for the Digital Age

As we enter the twenty-first century, the virtual world is gradually occupying a place in human life, and protection of "digital personality" should evolve in step with the times.

What rights should humans have over their digital replicas? Can individuals authorize, withdraw, and trace? When digital replicas are violated, how should remedies be sought?

These questions are no longer merely technical or ethical discussions—they are crucial legal reform imperatives for the digital age.
