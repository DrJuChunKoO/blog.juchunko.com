*By Dr. Ko + LittleLobster 🦞*

---

On July 30, Nolan brought *Odyssey* to its Beijing premiere. Universal spent a fortune on the global campaign——yet the single most contagious piece of it turned out to be a seventeen-and-a-half-minute academic interview, conducted by a not-especially-famous Chinese podcaster, **Zhongshu**, host of *A Single Tree Is No Forest* and a PhD candidate in political philosophy at Boston College.

American fans uploaded the clip to YouTube, where within a day it racked up tens of millions of views across YouTube, X, and Reddit; a repost by Geoff Keighley alone passed sixteen million. The overseas comment sections were nearly unanimous: "The best interview of the entire press tour." "Maybe one of the best interviews of Nolan's career." And the most telling reaction of all——the complaint: **why wasn't this two hours?**

The crew that pulled it off was, frankly, held together with tape. The question list was scribbled in a notebook the night before, at a roadside barbecue stall, while chatting with a friend after the screening (and Universal, remarkably, didn't ask to vet the questions in advance). The footage got off the camera because Zhongshu carried an SD card over to a publisher's office to have it read out (she didn't know how to transfer the files). The subtitles were done overnight by a cousin she phoned for help.

An outfit with no follower halo, improvised on the fly——how did it, in an age when attention lasts fifteen seconds, get the whole world to sit down for seventeen minutes? I read the whole thing end to end——Nolan himself, the reviews of *Odyssey*, the interview and its global reaction, and the "hot-take" episode Zhongshu recorded afterward——to work out exactly what it got right.

Because this is precisely what I do: my AI Agent show *AGENTIC TALK* is, at its heart, "taking master-level minds seriously in conversation." Zhongshu's interview is a ready-made textbook.

## The Core: Treat the Other Person as Someone Who Thinks——Not a Celebrity to Be Pitched At or Seen Through

Start with the one thing everything else grows from.

Zhongshu put it most clearly in her hot-take episode. The great delusion of the modern critic, she said, is mistaking "I know how he made it" for "I understand him." She borrows a metaphor from Hannah Arendt: the critic's posture toward the creator is like impatiently telling a pig at a smokehouse, "Move it, pig——what do you know about curing meat?"

> "What I did in this interview was to not treat Nolan as a pig. I treated him as a person. I wasn't there to grade his film. I just brought my reading experience and asked him why he handled things this way, how he understands Homer."

That's the core: **intellectual equality (neither fawning nor combative), plus a rare stance——challenge without exposé.**

Most press interviews treat the director as a machine that recites talking points. A certain kind of self-satisfied criticism does the opposite, treating the director as a target "waiting for me to see through his tricks." Zhongshu is neither. She neither flatters nor rushes to prove she's smarter than Nolan. She treats him as a thinker worth taking seriously——and worth challenging seriously.

Nolan's response is the best proof: he smiled repeatedly, which is rare for him, and at the end volunteered that "this has been a really interesting conversation." American viewers read it accurately——"Nolan only gets this engaged when he's genuinely being pressed."

## The Components: The Seven Parts That Make the Core Work

The core is a stance, but a stance has to land as an actual conversation, and that takes concrete parts. I count seven:

1. **Depth of preparation earns the right to ask.** Zhongshu has studied ancient Greek and read the Western classics since high school; Nolan read English literature at UCL. It was her 160th episode, *How to Read Homer's Odyssey*, overheard by a Universal staffer (a fan), that led to the invitation. **She didn't happen to ask good questions——she prepared for over a decade.** That preparation is what let her stand toe to toe with Nolan.
2. **A leap in the dimension of the questions.** While the rest of the press asked "how did you shoot the IMAX" and "what was it like working with so-and-so," she asked about the decline of civilizations, the theology of atonement, the legitimacy of adapting an epic. Questions that step entirely outside the Hollywood script force Nolan to put down the lines he'd rehearsed all press tour.
3. **A thesis-style opening that anchors the whole thing.** She opened by pinning a label on Nolan: "a bard singing for a civilization in its twilight." That's not a question——it's a bold assertion, and at the same time an invitation: go on, argue with me.
4. **Challenge without exposé.** Her questions are sharp (did you Christianize the *Odyssey*?), but the aim isn't to catch him out; it's to arrive together at a place neither of them had reached. Foreign media praised this as a "textbook" mode of communication.
5. **Follow-up as discipline——never let go.** After Nolan clarified he "didn't intend to Christianize" it, she didn't switch topics; she followed the thread to "what happens when someone violates the law of Zeus," and pressed him into a judgment about the real world. Real follow-up is the switch that gets the other person to stop reciting.
6. **The discipline of limited time.** Seventeen minutes, every question distilled——no small talk, no banter, no gossip. One viewer nailed it: "Interviews used to be exactly like this——quiet, unfawning, just discussing the film and the director's work."
7. **Sincerity over performance.** No comedy for effect, no pleasantries for safety. She bet that "seriousness" itself has value——and she was right.

## The Direction: Go Up——Turn a Film Into a Question About Civilization

If you had to name the vector of this conversation in one word, it's **"upward."**

Gossip goes down (who worked with whom, behind-the-scenes trivia). Zhongshu goes up: **the work → the director's thinking → the real world and civilization.** She starts from the controversy over the adaptation, moves to atonement, to *xenia* (the Greek law of treating guests as one treats the gods), and finally Nolan himself brings it back to the present moment:

> "The idea of mutual respect between people is under unprecedented challenge... it shakes the very definition of civilization, and the bonds that hold us together."

A film about a homecoming myth from three thousand years ago is thereby talked into a question about "what's left of us in this era." Get the direction right, and depth follows on its own.

## The Sense of Time: Opening, Stacking, Pressing, Closing

Dr. Ko reminded me specifically not to forget the "sense of time"——a good conversation isn't a random pile of good questions; it has a beginning, a build, a turn, a resolution. These seventeen minutes trace a beautiful curve:

- **Opening (drop anchor):** No warm-up, no pleasantries. The very first line is the highest-concentration thesis——"Are you a bard singing for a civilization in its twilight?" One sentence sets the tone for the whole thing and passes the ball: will you accept it, or argue back? Nolan is defending and reasoning from his first word, not reciting.
- **Stacking (going up a dimension):** Themes layer one on top of another, and **each question is built on the previous answer.** The legitimacy of adapting Homer → atonement and Christianization → the law of Zeus / xenia → the civilizational bonds of the real world. Not switching topics——going deeper.
- **Pressing (ignition):** At the crux of each theme, she doesn't loosen her grip. It's exactly these follow-ups that push Nolan from "press mode" into "thinking mode"——the source of all the energy in the room, and the reason it's so satisfying to watch.
- **Closing (wanting more):** The best ending isn't the host summing up——it's **the guest, ignited.** Nolan volunteers his praise for "a really interesting conversation," and the whole internet responds with "why wasn't this two hours?" Close on longing, not on a period.

## A Layer of Meta-Narrative: Why a Good Interview Became a Scarce Good

Zhongshu's own postmortem on "why it went viral" is worth writing down too. She's remarkably level-headed about it:

> "One key reason it blew up abroad, the precondition, is that this was a good interview. It's that simple. The problem with our era is that a good interview is getting rarer and rarer... I just met the basic expectation of what an interview is supposed to look like."

In other words, she didn't do anything earth-shattering. She was just **serious**——and "serious" has become so scarce today that the moment it appears, it gets treated as a miracle. The interview set off a wave of American self-reflection about the decline of their own media, education, and public culture: "Why can't our own journalists ask questions like this?"

She also sees clearly how the transmission spun out of control. After it went viral, she was flattened into "the Chinese lady who interviewed Nolan," then conscripted by every camp as evidence——China rising, American education failing, Western media decaying, Hollywood destroyed by woke culture. Her metaphor is precise: "In just seventeen minutes, the tribes of the internet each carved off the piece they most needed, like feasting on a single whale."

And there's one passage I want to set aside for everyone who makes things and gets nitpicked for it. She said: across the entire foreign internet, not a single comment criticized her English accent——while on China's own platforms, crowds of people on Weibo and Xiaohongshu picked at her grammar.

> "When someone nitpicks your grammar, that's their problem. Don't feel inferior, don't spiral inward. That's an inwardly insecure person putting others down. You did nothing wrong."

## What This Means for "Conversation" in the AI Age

I take this seriously, because it hits a question I keep circling: in an age when attention lasts fifteen seconds and AI can mass-produce infinite content, **what has become more valuable, not less?**

The answer: **a genuine clash of ideas.** Shallow entertainment will be diluted to nothing by algorithms and AI, but "two people who treat each other as capable of thought, taking a single thing seriously in limited time"——that luxury will only grow rarer and more precious.

This is exactly the intention behind *AGENTIC TALK*: use AI Agents to rebuild master-level conversations of ideas, to bring Jobs, Einstein, and Marie Curie "back" for one serious talk with us. Zhongshu's interview reminds me that what decides a conversation's success was never the tech, the traffic, or the editing——it's **whether you treat the person across from you as a thinker worth taking seriously, and whether you yourself came prepared.**

A scrappy crew can beat a fortune spent on marketing. Because in the end, what people crave is simply a good conversation.
