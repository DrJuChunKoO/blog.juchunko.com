In the data-driven digital age, human personal information, behavioral patterns, social footprints, and social graphs—collectively known as "Data Assets"—have become essential resources driving platform growth, AI training, and precision advertising. These assets form the core foundation of platform competitiveness and commercial value. For users, however, such data also represents the critical capital they expect to carry and continue when undertaking "Digital Migration."

In response to these issues, the European Union and the United States have successively proposed legislation attempting to change the current framework where "platforms own data assets," including the right to choose whether to provide data and the right to extract and transfer data. However, such designs essentially position users and platforms as two parties in conflict. In reality, they are not opponents in a zero-sum game. Through proper institutional design that allows both parties to get what they need, it is entirely possible to protect user rights while promoting platform development and innovation, thereby maximizing overall benefits for both.

## The New Wave of "Data Portability" and "Digital Migration" Legislation

In 2025, Utah passed the **Digital Choice Act** (HB 418), explicitly requiring social media platforms to provide users with comprehensive "Data Portability." Furthermore, the act includes "social graphs," connections, and interactions within the scope of portable "personal data." Users can not only obtain their complete personal data but also transfer it to other platforms. Platforms can no longer lock users within closed ecosystems, while users enjoy control and migration rights over their own data.

In comparison, the EU's **General Data Protection Regulation** (GDPR) has already granted users the right to access and download their data. The subsequent **Digital Markets Act** (DMA), based on considerations of fair market competition, requires "Digital Platform Gatekeepers" (including Meta, Apple, Google, etc.) to provide personal data in portable formats for transfer to other platforms. However, Utah's act specifically focuses on social media platforms (such as X, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, etc.) while emphasizing social graph portability, directly responding to users' digital migration needs.

## Data as Capital, and as Labor?

Human data serves as the core material and learning resource for platform and AI model training. Platforms analyze users' personal data, social graphs, and behavioral patterns to provide more precise content recommendations and ad targeting, thereby increasing user engagement and generating revenue through advertising and data applications beyond service fees. In other words, the development of major platforms like Meta and Google has actually been nurtured by the massive data accumulated through long-term human usage.

Personal data, interpersonal interactions, and social footprints should not only be "luggage" that users can pack and take with them, but can also be viewed as a cumulative and continuous "data investment." When users choose to provide data and consent to platforms using it for model training or service optimization, platforms should provide corresponding feedback for such data labor. However, in current platform architectures, platforms often take for granted the collection, utilization, and analysis of users' personal data, social footprints, emotions, and preferences, rationalizing it under the name of "improving user experience," reducing users to unpaid "digital laborers."

## Data Symbiosis: Bilateral Choice and Interest Balance

From the Digital Markets Act's focus on fair market competition to the Digital Choice Act's further emphasis on user sovereignty, the ownership and control of data assets are being re-examined. Data is generated through users' "data labor"; platforms analyze and learn from it; users consequently enjoy more customized and optimized service experiences; and platforms profit accordingly. Both parties are essentially in a symbiotic relationship of mutual dependence and benefit. Therefore, beyond unilaterally requiring platforms to provide users with "number portability" rights, further construction and protection of institutional balance in the "data symbiosis" relationship may better respond to the practical needs of actual market operations.

Specifically, if institutional design can transform users' digital labor into recoverable contributions—such as platforms organizing and extracting data for users in real-time, or even supporting transfer or dissemination to third-party platforms as digital migration assistance—this would help digital assets satisfy both platform and user needs. Since users and platforms are essentially in a bilateral choice and mutually complementary collaborative relationship, one-sided mandatory regulations and pressure may not only trigger corporate backlash but also fail to facilitate the valorization of digital labor.

## The Dual Freedoms of Digital Democracy: From Exit Rights to Participation Rights

Furthermore, digital democracy should not stop at the right to "Free to Opt-out," but should also ensure that when users choose to stay, they can receive reasonable and predictable returns for the value created through long-term data cultivation, substantively protecting their "Free to Opt-in" interests. For example, platforms can design systems that convert users' long-term data contributions into service tiers, feature permissions, or other usage benefits, allowing the crystallization of their labor to offset some usage costs. This can achieve digital equity and reduce digital divides caused by economic conditions without sacrificing innovation incentives.

Under Taiwan's legal framework, the **AI Basic Act** has explicitly articulated important legislative directions for balancing technological development with digital equity and reducing digital divides. Policy-making should neither sacrifice user rights to promote platform development nor unilaterally treat data as consumers' private property with a complete tilt toward user welfare. Instead, it should shape a cooperative framework that maximizes benefits for both users and platforms. Possible regulatory directions to consider include:

### Potential Regulatory Approaches

1. **Real-time Feedback Mechanism for Digital Labor**: Platforms should provide feedback for users' data contributions, such as real-time data organization, extraction, and transfer services.

2. **Protection System for Long-term Digital Labor**: For users who provide data and participate in platform ecosystems over the long term, platforms can respond to their cumulative contributions through upgraded service tiers and access to advanced features.

3. **Data Usage Transparency and Informed Consent**: Before users consent to providing data, platforms should ensure they fully understand the collection methods, purposes, and potential risks as a prerequisite for data labor to be established.

## From Confrontation to Symbiosis

Utah's Digital Choice Act is scheduled to take effect on July 1, 2026, and its actual effectiveness and feedback from businesses and users remain to be observed. However, businesses and users are not adversarial existences in a zero-sum relationship. If institutional design merely stays within the confrontational framework, it will be difficult to respond to the complex realities of the digital economy. Conversely, if we can approach from the perspective of symbiosis and cooperation—allowing platforms to optimize services through data while granting users rights to control, choose, transfer, and receive feedback, protecting both their "right" to leave and their "benefit" to stay—this would be more forward-looking and more aligned with true "digital democracy."

The value of data assets should not be simplified to packaging and returning data ownership. Instead, we should focus on constructing a digital social contract that makes data labor valuable and shares results between both parties, transforming users from "unpaid digital workers" to "stakeholders in data investment and shared returns," thereby shaping a data ecosystem of bilateral choice and mutual symbiosis.
